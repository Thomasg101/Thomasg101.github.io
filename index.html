Start with the frame.

Take the cladding off and a building is a load path: slab to beam, beam to column, column to caisson. I have worked on it from both ends, in the analysis model and in the ground.

Lateral drift, exaggerated so the deflected shape is visible. An illustrative model, not a design.

  • Steel frame analysis and verification A two-bay sub-frame solved in SAP2000, then re-solved with a direct-stiffness solver I wrote. The two disagreed, and the case study explains why.
  • Deep foundations at Pape Station Coordinated diaphragm walls, caissons, soldier piles and lagging, and load testing on the Ontario Line.

Then the skin.

The envelope decides where the heat goes. At Entuitive I modelled junction details in Flixo, THERM and HEAT3 to get effective U-values and linear and point transmittances for BC Energy Step Code, NECB and ASHRAE 90.1, most of them twice: as built and as retrofitted.

Colder surfaceWarmer surface

As built: slab edges, balconies and parapets run straight through the insulation, so that is where the heat leaves.

An exterior thermogram of the model on a cold night. Illustrative, not project data.

  • Envelope thermal-bridge studies 2D steady-state studies of roof-to-wall and balcony-slab junctions across existing and retrofit insulation.
  • Restoration field review at Brown Beattie Waterproofing and concrete repair inspections, from tender review through substantial performance.

Then the data.

A project leaves behind thousands of site photos and reports that nobody can search. I build the tools that read them: vision models that tag what is in a photo, hybrid retrieval that finds it, and language models that draft from cited sources.
